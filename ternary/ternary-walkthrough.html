Ternary Transformer Walkthrough

Follow three tokens through the model, step by step. Every multiply is marked by kind, so you can see where ternary weights save work and where they cannot.

data × ternary weights data × data lookup or element-wise (no big multiply)

Where the work goes

Multiply-adds per token, per layer, in the model we actually trained (256 numbers per token, feed-forward 1,024, context 256 tokens, so each token looks back at about 128 others on average).

Not counted above: the output layer (256 × 4,096 = 1,048,576 per token, once per model, not per layer). In our Phase 5 model and in BitNet it stays decimal; in Phase 6 it is ternary. In big models it is a small share.

Where a breakthrough could come from

In training, about 31% of the work is the weight-gradient multiply (saved data × gradient). Ternary weights do not touch it. These are ideas to test, not results.

Idea 1 · untested

Estimate the weight gradient from fewer tokens

Your flip rule only needs to know which weights to flip, not the exact gradient. Computing it from, say, 1 in 8 tokens would cut that 31% roughly 8×, if flips stay good enough.

Idea 2 · untested

Low-bit saved data and gradients

The saved data is already 8-bit in our layers. Making the gradient low-bit too turns the weight-gradient multiply into cheap integer work. Ternary data is the extreme version.

Idea 3 · needs kernels

Ternary kernels for the other 62%

Forward and gradient-for-data are data × ternary. A kernel built for training would turn those into adds and subtracts. Today our code still uses float multiplies there.

Toy numbers are computed live on this page. Simplifications in the toy: one attention head, no normalisation, no 8-bit rounding of the data. Position is added to x, as in classic GPT; nanochat rotates q and k instead.
